# Preventing Muscle Loss on Ozempic and Other GLP-1 Medications

GLP-1 medications such as semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) have changed what rapid, sustained weight loss looks like for millions of people. But the appetite suppression that makes these drugs so effective has a downside that gets far less attention than it should: when you eat dramatically less, your body does not lose only fat. A meaningful share of the weight can come from lean muscle mass, and that muscle is much harder to rebuild later than it was to lose.

**The fix is not complicated, even if it takes consistency: eat enough protein, keep lifting or doing resistance exercise, and avoid losing weight faster than your body can spare.** None of this requires fighting the medication or eating more than feels comfortable. This article is educational, not medical advice — talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing your medication, diet, or exercise routine, especially if you have an existing health condition.

## Why GLP-1 Weight Loss Puts Muscle at Risk

GLP-1 drugs work by slowing digestion and reducing appetite, often leading people to eat a few hundred fewer calories a day without trying. That is the whole point of the medication, and it works. The problem is that any significant calorie deficit, whatever causes it, pulls some energy from muscle tissue as well as fat stores — and the less protein and resistance exercise you get, the more of that loss comes from muscle.

Clinical research on GLP-1-driven weight loss has found that a substantial portion of the weight lost, commonly cited in the range of a quarter to a third of total weight, can be lean mass rather than fat. That is a higher share than typically seen with more gradual, diet-only weight loss. Several factors make this risk worse:

- **Fast weight loss.** The faster the weight comes off, the larger the share that tends to come from muscle.
- **Low protein intake.** A smaller appetite often means smaller meals across the board, including protein-rich ones.
- **Little or no resistance training.** Muscle that is not being used and challenged gets deprioritized when your body is short on energy.
- **Older age.** Muscle is naturally harder to maintain as we age, so the same deficit can cost more lean mass later in life.

Losing some muscle does not mean the medication is a mistake. It means the two habits below deserve real attention alongside it.

## Habit One: Prioritize Protein at Every Meal

Protein is the raw material your body uses to maintain and repair muscle, and it is also the macronutrient most likely to fall short when your appetite drops. On GLP-1 medications, aim to get enough protein even if your total food volume is smaller.

A widely used target for people actively losing weight who want to protect muscle is roughly **1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day** (about 0.7 to 1.0 grams per pound), spread across your meals rather than loaded into one. For a 75kg (165lb) person, that works out to roughly 120-165 grams a day. If that sounds like a lot compared with how much you are currently eating, raise it gradually rather than all at once, since nausea and early fullness are common side effects of these medications.

Practical ways to hit a smaller but protein-dense target:

- Eat protein first on your plate, before vegetables or starches, while your appetite is at its highest for the meal.
- Choose naturally protein-dense foods — eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, fish, poultry, tofu, and legumes — over foods that are mostly carbohydrate or fat.
- Keep a protein shake or bar on hand for days when solid food feels unappealing; convenience matters when appetite is low.
- Space protein across three or four smaller meals instead of trying to eat one large one, which can be uncomfortable on these medications.

## Habit Two: Keep Resistance Training a Priority

Protein alone will not protect muscle if it is never asked to do anything. Muscle tissue responds to the signal of being used; without that signal, your body treats it as less essential and is more willing to let it go during a calorie deficit. Resistance training is what tells your body which tissue to keep.

You do not need an elaborate program. **Two to three resistance sessions a week that work all the major muscle groups** — legs, back, chest, shoulders, arms, and core — is enough to substantially blunt muscle loss during GLP-1-driven weight loss, according to the general strength-training guidance clinicians commonly cite for this situation. Bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, or light-to-moderate dumbbells are all legitimate options if a gym is not accessible or heavy lifting feels like too much right now.

A simple weekly structure:

1. **Two full-body sessions** covering squats or lunges, a pushing movement, a pulling movement, and core work.
2. **One optional extra session** focused on whatever feels weakest, or a longer walk with light carrying (a backpack or grocery bags) for added resistance.
3. **Daily movement in between** — walking remains valuable for general health and energy, but it does not replace resistance training for preserving muscle.

If fatigue or appetite is a bigger barrier on a given week, shorter 15-20 minute sessions twice a week still beat skipping resistance training entirely. Consistency matters more than intensity here.

## Watch Your Rate of Loss, Not Just the Scale

Because faster weight loss tends to cost more muscle, the pace of your loss is itself a tool for protecting it. Many clinicians suggest aiming for roughly **0.5 to 1% of body weight per week** as a sustainable range, though your prescribing doctor may set a different target based on your specific situation and starting weight. If the scale is moving noticeably faster than that and you are also skipping meals or struggling to eat enough protein, it is worth raising with your care team — sometimes a dose adjustment or a deliberate focus on food quality makes a real difference.

The scale cannot tell you whether weight lost is fat or muscle. If you can, track a second signal alongside it:

- **Strength trends** — are your lifts or reps holding steady or improving, even as your weight drops?
- **How clothes fit** around the waist versus the arms and legs, which can hint at proportionally more fat loss versus muscle loss.
- **Energy for daily tasks** — carrying groceries, climbing stairs, getting up from the floor.
- **Body composition scans**, if accessible through your clinic, which give a more direct read than a bathroom scale.

## Other Supporting Habits

A few additional habits make protein and training more effective rather than fighting against low appetite:

- **Stay hydrated.** Reduced thirst cues and slower digestion can make dehydration easier to miss, which worsens fatigue and can be confused with hunger.
- **Don't skip meals entirely.** Small, frequent, protein-forward meals are usually easier to manage than trying to force down one large meal.
- **Get enough sleep.** Poor sleep increases muscle breakdown and makes high-intensity exercise feel harder, undermining both pillars above.
- **Ask about micronutrients.** Eating less food overall can mean less of everything, including vitamins and minerals; your doctor may suggest bloodwork or a basic multivitamin.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will I definitely lose muscle on a GLP-1 medication?

Not necessarily, and definitely not to the same degree for everyone. Research shows lean mass loss is common during rapid, medication-assisted weight loss, but people who eat enough protein and keep resistance training consistently lose proportionally less muscle than those who do neither.

### How much protein is too much while on a GLP-1 medication?

For most healthy adults, protein intake up to roughly 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight is well within normal, safe ranges. If you have kidney disease or another condition affecting protein metabolism, talk to your doctor about an appropriate target before increasing your intake significantly.

### Can I do cardio instead of strength training to protect muscle?

Cardio is valuable for heart health and overall fitness, but it does not send the same "keep this muscle" signal that resistance training does. If you can only do one type of exercise, resistance training is the better choice for preserving muscle specifically; ideally, you include both.

### What if solid food and protein feel impossible some days?

This is common, especially early on a GLP-1 medication or after a dose increase. Liquid options — protein shakes, smoothies, soups with added protein — are often easier to manage than solid food and still count toward your daily target. If nausea or early fullness is severe or persistent, let your prescribing doctor know, since a dose adjustment may help.

Preserving muscle on a GLP-1 medication comes down to two habits practiced consistently: enough protein, spread across the day, and resistance training a couple of times a week.
